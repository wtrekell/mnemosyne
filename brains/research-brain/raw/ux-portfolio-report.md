# An evidence-led UX portfolio workflow

**Research date: October 23, 2026**  
**Scope:** Claude AI, Claude Design, and Claude Code for an experienced designer’s portfolio.  
**Export note:** This is the full report from the preceding response, not a new research update. Citations are rendered as direct source links so the document can be used outside the conversation.

## 1. Executive recommendation

Use **Claude AI to organize and challenge the evidence, Claude Design to explore its presentation, and Claude Code to implement and maintain the chosen experience**. This division is feasible with the documented products, but the designer—not the tools—must approve historical claims, attribution, publication rights, design decisions, and release readiness. ([Claude Design guide](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/14604416-get-started-with-claude-design)) ([Claude Code quickstart](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/quickstart)) ([Claude Projects](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/9517075-what-are-projects))

The portfolio should be a **curated explanation of professional judgment**, not a comprehensive archive or a polished reconstruction of an ideal process. Research involving 204 UX hiring professionals supports showing role, constraints, reasoning, iteration, and value—not merely finished screens; that research is from 2019, so it is a durable starting point rather than a measurement of the entire 2026 market. ([NN/g, 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/))

### Recommended starting scope

The following is a **proposed implementation strategy**, not a universal hiring requirement:

- Define one primary role direction and, if necessary, one adjacent direction.
- Inventory the archive before writing case studies.
- Develop two complementary, evidence-rich cases initially; add another only if it answers an important question the first two cannot.
- Give each case a concise orientation layer and optional deeper evidence.
- Keep a separate, tailored presentation for interviews.
- Choose the simplest publishing system the designer can maintain.
- Test whether intended reviewers understand fit and contribution before investing heavily in visual novelty.

The initial two-case scope is a time-management choice. NN/g’s older guidance explicitly says project count is not important in itself and suggests concentrating on one or two substantial projects when time is limited; it does not establish a hiring threshold. ([NN/g, 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/))

### What the portfolio can influence

A portfolio can make relevant experience easier to find, explain individual contribution, supply material for evaluation, and support interview discussion. GitLab’s published 2026 process explicitly separates recruiter qualification checks, hiring-manager portfolio examination, and cross-functional discussion. ([GitLab UX Architect, 2026](https://handbook.gitlab.com/job-description-library/product/ux-architect/))

It cannot guarantee employment, create missing experience, override a hard role requirement, or control headcount and hiring decisions. A March 2026 interview with HubSpot recruiter Steph McDonald describes both role-specific screening requirements and constrained hiring; this is one recruiter’s account, not a market-wide estimate. ([Steph McDonald interview, 2026](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/podcasts/ux-hiring-insights-steph-mcdonald-ux-design-recruiter-at-hubspot/))

**Assess the portfolio first as a communication product:** can reviewers accurately explain the designer’s fit, contribution, decisions, and evidence? Track job-search progress separately, without assuming a portfolio change caused an interview or offer.

### Evidence labels

Throughout this report:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Documented capability** | Established by official product documentation; not a guarantee of output quality |
| **Documented practice** | An attributable employer process or reviewer perspective |
| **Practitioner demonstration** | A firsthand account of building something; not evidence of hiring effectiveness |
| **Proposed method** | A recommended workflow, template, acceptance criterion, or experiment |
| **Unresolved** | Not established by the available research |

No interviews, usability sessions, portfolio builds, or hiring-outcome experiments were conducted for this report.

## 2. What the 2026 hiring evidence supports—and does not

### The market requires context

The available evidence does **not** justify describing the entire 2026 UX market as uniformly growing, collapsing, or favoring one portfolio style.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 5% growth for the combined category of web developers and digital designers between 2025 and 2035. That August 26, 2026 publication is a broad occupational projection—not a count of current UX vacancies, applicant competition, or senior-design opportunities. ([BLS, 2026](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/web-developers.htm))

McDonald’s March 30, 2026 account describes fewer openings and heightened expectations in the hiring she observes. Treat that as attributable recruiting experience, not a representative labor-market study. ([Steph McDonald interview, 2026](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/podcasts/ux-hiring-insights-steph-mcdonald-ux-design-recruiter-at-hubspot/))

A frequently cited UX Design Institute hiring report is actually labeled **2024** and draws on more than 500 UX professionals and hiring managers. It should not be relabeled as 2026 evidence. ([UX Design Institute, 2024](https://www.uxdesigninstitute.com/state-of-ux-hiring))

**Practical implication:** select target roles from actual openings, then use the portfolio to demonstrate relevant evidence. Do not rebuild around generalized claims about “what the market wants.”

### Recruiter screening

McDonald describes looking for relevant experience on the résumé before opening the portfolio, then examining craft, impact, iteration, and usability. These are her reported practices. ([Steph McDonald interview, 2026](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/podcasts/ux-hiring-insights-steph-mcdonald-ux-design-recruiter-at-hubspot/))

GitLab’s UX Architect process assigns its recruiter call to experience, basic qualifications, and communication; deeper examination occurs later. ([GitLab UX Architect, 2026](https://handbook.gitlab.com/job-description-library/product/ux-architect/))

**Proposed recruiter questions for the portfolio to answer:**

- What role is this designer seeking?
- What relevant product problems has the designer worked on?
- What level and scope of contribution are supported?
- Which project should be opened first for this vacancy?
- Are the claims consistent with the résumé?
- Can the reviewer access the work, résumé, and contact route?
- Is restricted work explained without creating an access dead end?

The homepage should help answer these questions without requiring a full case-study read. This is an architectural recommendation derived from the screening distinction—not a claim that every recruiter follows the same sequence.

### Hiring-manager evaluation

GitLab’s 2026 design-hiring guidance identifies design craft, user-centered thinking, collaboration, and iteration as core competencies, while varying emphasis by assignment model. ([GitLab design hiring, 2026](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/upstream-studios/product-design/hiring-product-designers/))

Its UX Architect process examines decision-making, methodologies, scalable architecture, cross-product work, and balancing UX with product and engineering constraints. ([GitLab UX Architect, 2026](https://handbook.gitlab.com/job-description-library/product/ux-architect/))

**Proposed hiring-manager questions:**

- Was the problem understood and framed appropriately?
- What did the designer personally own?
- Which evidence informed important decisions?
- What alternatives were considered?
- How were constraints negotiated?
- What changed through iteration?
- What demonstrates interaction, visual, content, or systems craft?
- What happened afterward, and how reliable is that account?
- Does the demonstrated scope fit this role?

Do not reduce this to “thinking matters, visuals do not.” McDonald explicitly values cohesive craft, while GitLab includes craft alongside other competencies. ([Steph McDonald interview, 2026](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/podcasts/ux-hiring-insights-steph-mcdonald-ux-design-recruiter-at-hubspot/)) ([GitLab design hiring, 2026](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/upstream-studios/product-design/hiring-product-designers/))

### Portfolio interviews

NN/g’s June 26, 2020 guidance distinguishes a portfolio’s screening function from its role in supporting an interview. That distinction remains useful, but the source does not prescribe one interview format. ([NN/g interview guidance, 2020](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/ux-portfolios-interviews/))

GitLab’s current UX Architect process includes detailed discussion of portfolio projects, collaboration, constraints, and organizational influence. ([GitLab UX Architect, 2026](https://handbook.gitlab.com/job-description-library/product/ux-architect/))

**Proposed interview questions:**

- Why was this problem worth addressing?
- What did the designer know at the time?
- Why was one option chosen over another?
- What would a collaborator describe differently?
- What evidence contradicted the original assumption?
- What remains uncertain?
- What would be done differently now?

Prepare a presentation that supports discussion rather than narrating every paragraph of the website.

### Claims not supported

This research does not establish:

- A universal portfolio review time.
- An ideal number of projects.
- One mandatory publishing platform.
- That all applicant-tracking systems inspect portfolio websites.
- A universal penalty for AI-assisted writing or implementation.
- That missing business metrics make a project worthless.
- That custom code improves hiring outcomes.
- That the exact three-tool workflow increases interviews or offers.

McDonald accepts AI-assisted polishing while criticizing unreviewed, misleading automated applications. That is evidence against a blanket “AI use is automatically disqualifying” claim, not proof that every employer welcomes it. ([Steph McDonald interview, 2026](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/podcasts/ux-hiring-insights-steph-mcdonald-ux-design-recruiter-at-hubspot/))

### Verified handoffs

The official Claude Design guide currently documents:

- `/design-sync` for bringing design-system context from Claude Code.
- `/design` for creating, editing, importing, and synchronizing design work.
- Handoff to a local coding agent or Claude Code Web.
- Exports including ZIP, PDF, PPTX, Google Slides, and standalone HTML. ([Claude Design guide](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/14604416-get-started-with-claude-design))

These are documented capabilities, **not evidence that every account, export, or project will behave identically**. Test a small, non-sensitive example before choosing a handoff route.

Do not infer that design synchronization also synchronizes the evidence ledger, legal permissions, approved wording, or every conversation. Maintain those explicitly.

**Fallback if a command or integration is unavailable:** export the approved design and supply Claude Code with the approved text, layout specification, tokens, interaction notes, and sanitized assets. Label this a **manual handoff**, not automatic synchronization.

### Coding knowledge

For a custom implementation, the proposed minimum is the ability—or access to help—to:

- Understand files, folders, Git changes, and a local preview.
- Review a proposed change before accepting it.
- Run the project’s documented build and checks.
- Restore a known working version.
- Understand what becomes public during deployment.

Claude Code can reduce implementation effort; its documented quickstart still assumes a code project, authentication, and a working development environment. ([Claude Code quickstart](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/quickstart))

### Permissions and data handling

**Uploading and publishing require separate decisions.** A file approved for an internal presentation is not automatically approved for an AI service or public website.

Official [Claude Code data-use documentation](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/data-usage), checked October 23, distinguishes consumer model-improvement settings from commercial terms: consumer training use depends on settings; commercial content is not used for generative-model training by default, subject to documented exceptions and opt-ins.

The same documentation states that local Claude Code sends model-related data over the network and stores local session transcripts. “Runs locally” therefore must not be interpreted as “the material stays only on this Mac.” [Official data-flow and retention documentation](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/data-usage)

Retention is product- and account-dependent. Saved commercial conversations can remain to support continued use; deleting them triggers a documented deletion window, with exceptions, rather than proving immediate erasure. ([Anthropic organizational retention](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/7996866-how-long-do-you-store-my-organization-s-data))

**Recommended controls:**

- Keep original confidential sources outside the AI workspace.
- Upload only explicitly approved, minimized derivatives.
- Inspect consumer model-improvement settings before use.
- Do not treat “training disabled” as permission to upload restricted work.
- Review connectors before granting repository, storage, or account access.
- Use a dedicated portfolio working directory.
- Inspect permissions rather than assuming every action requires approval.
- Keep deployment credentials out of prompts and source files.

Claude Code’s official [permissions documentation](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/permissions), checked October 23, describes Manual, Plan, automatic, and bypass modes. It also distinguishes instructions from enforced permissions: a sentence in `CLAUDE.md` is not a security boundary.

## 4. Project audit and selection method

### Inventory before narrative

NN/g’s August 2019 process begins with a project inventory and prioritization against the work sought. That remains a useful sequencing principle, although it is older guidance. ([NN/g, 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/))

**Proposed process:**

1. List projects without deciding which deserve case studies.
2. Record known facts and uncertainty separately.
3. Locate evidence without uploading the whole archive.
4. Record permissions and restrictions.
5. Identify the project’s strongest decision story.
6. Estimate the effort required to make it publishable.
7. Select a complementary set.

The audit should reveal both **what happened** and **what can be credibly shown**.

### Inventory template

Use one row per project in a spreadsheet, or one record per project in a document.

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Project ID | Neutral identifier; avoid confidential names |
| Period | Exact or approximate dates, with confidence |
| Product context | Audience, environment, and relevant complexity |
| User problem | What was difficult, risky, or unmet |
| Stakes | Why addressing it mattered |
| Designer role | Formal role if authorized; otherwise functional description |
| Individual contribution | Specific actions, artifacts, and decisions owned |
| Team responsibilities | What research, product, engineering, content, or other collaborators owned |
| Constraints | Technical, operational, organizational, time, legal, accessibility |
| Tradeoffs | What improved, what was deferred, and what cost was accepted |
| Evidence available | Research, files, notes, versions, tickets, tests, approved screenshots |
| Alternatives | Options actually considered at the time |
| Decisions influenced | What changed because of the designer’s contribution |
| Status | Shipped, tested, proposed, discontinued, or unknown |
| Outcomes | Observations, measurements, feedback, or unresolved questions |
| Outcome evidence | Source, period, scope, and limitations |
| Permissions | Publication authorization and AI-processing authorization separately |
| Restrictions | NDA, policy, participant data, customer details, internal material |
| Target-role relevance | Which requirements this project demonstrates |
| Missing information | Facts or artifacts still needed |
| Publishability effort | Retrieval, clarification, redaction, writing, reconstruction, review |
| Candidate use | Full case, short example, interview-only if authorized, or exclude |

**Stop condition:** unclear rights or dangerous source material means “hold,” not “send to Claude for anonymization.”

### A transparent selection rubric

This is a **proposed prioritization aid**, not a validated predictor of hiring success.

First apply non-negotiable gates:

| Gate | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| Rights | Intended use is permitted; uncertainty has an owner and resolution path |
| Truth | The central story can be told without invented responsibilities, research, or outcomes |
| Attribution | Individual contribution can be distinguished from team output |
| Defensibility | The designer can explain the important decisions and limitations |

Then score eligible projects from 0 to 3: **0 absent, 1 weak, 2 adequate, 3 strong**.

| Dimension | Weight | Question |
|---|---:|---|
| Target-role relevance | 3 | Does it demonstrate capabilities needed for the intended work? |
| Decision evidence | 3 | Can important choices and alternatives be supported? |
| Contribution clarity | 3 | Can personal ownership be described precisely? |
| Evidence strength | 3 | Are sources reliable enough for the claims proposed? |
| Craft or systems depth | 2 | Can the relevant quality be inspected? |
| Complementarity | 2 | Does it add something missing from other selected cases? |
| Reflection value | 1 | Can it reveal learning without retrospective fabrication? |
| Preparation feasibility | 2 | Can it be made publishable within available time? |

Record a short rationale beside each score. The weighted total helps expose assumptions; it must not overrule a failed gate or erase a compelling exception.

**Selection rule:** choose the smallest set that covers the priority role requirements with credible evidence. Do not automatically choose the highest individual scores if the resulting cases repeat the same story.

### Handling an uneven archive

| Archive condition | Proposed treatment | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Older work with valuable judgment | Date it; explain the relevant decision and what still transfers | Implying it demonstrates current tool fluency |
| Recent work without outcome data | Show decisions, delivery status, and what remains unmeasured | Inventing a business benefit |
| Failed or redirected work | Explain the original aim, evidence, decision to change, and learning | Recasting cancellation as a proven success |
| Small contribution to a large initiative | Bound the case around the contribution actually owned | Presenting the whole initiative as personal work |
| Screenshots prohibited | Use approved explanations, permitted diagrams, or a separately authorized reconstruction | Assuming a renamed interface is publishable |
| Aged visual style | Preserve historical context; optionally show a clearly separate current critique | Quietly modernizing historical screenshots |
| Missing research artifacts | Describe only what is supported; label recollection | Generating personas or quotations to fill gaps |
| No reliable outcome metric | Explain what changed and what cannot be concluded | Replacing missing metrics with vague “significant improvement” language |

Unimplemented alternatives can be useful evidence of working within constraints; NN/g’s older research-based guidance specifically includes decisions not pursued. ([NN/g, 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/))

## 5. Portfolio architecture and case-study strategy

### Three review depths

The following architecture is **proposed**, based on the documented distinction between initial screening, detailed evaluation, and interview discussion. ([NN/g interview guidance, 2020](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/ux-portfolios-interviews/)) ([GitLab UX Architect, 2026](https://handbook.gitlab.com/job-description-library/product/ux-architect/))

| Depth | Reviewer need | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation | “Is there potentially relevant experience here?” | Positioning, project cards, role and scope, résumé, contact |
| Evaluation | “Does the work support the apparent fit?” | Case summary, decisions, evidence, contribution, limitations |
| Discussion | “Can the designer explain and defend the work?” | Presentation, selected artifacts, alternatives, deeper questions |

Do not duplicate the entire story at every depth. Repeat the essential facts consistently, then progressively add explanation.

### Recommended content architecture

| Area | Proposed content | Acceptance criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Homepage | Clear role direction and evidence-backed positioning | A reviewer can describe the intended role without guessing |
| Selected work | Descriptive titles, context, contribution, status | Projects can be chosen for relevance rather than thumbnail appeal |
| Case-study summary | Problem, scope, personal contribution, key decision, outcome status | The project makes sense before deep reading |
| Full case study | Evidence-led account of consequential choices | Important claims have support or explicit uncertainty |
| About | Working approach and authorized professional context | Adds useful context without repeating the résumé |
| Résumé | Accessible link and downloadable version where appropriate | Opens without a request or unexpected login |
| Contact | A deliberate, tested route | The designer can be contacted without exposing unnecessary personal information |
| Interview materials | Tailored presentation and approved backup artifacts | Supports conversation and works without the live site |

Positioning should be an **index to evidence**, not an unsupported slogan.

For example, a statement about “complex workflow design” should lead to an inspectable workflow decision. A claim about “systems leadership” needs evidence of influence or leverage beyond an isolated screen.

### Flexible case-study structure

Use common orientation fields, but let the substantive sections follow the project.

1. **Problem and stakes:** who faced what problem, and why it mattered.
2. **Role and scope:** personal contribution, collaborators, exclusions.
3. **Constraints:** conditions shaping the available choices.
4. **Evidence:** what was known at the time and how it was obtained.
5. **Decisions:** choices, alternatives, rationale, accepted tradeoffs.
6. **Iteration:** what changed and what prompted the change.
7. **Outcome and uncertainty:** status, observations, measurements, limits.
8. **Reflection:** what is learned now, explicitly separated from historical knowledge.

A systems case might center on adoption and governance; a discovery case on uncertainty reduction; an interaction case on behavior and iteration. Do not force every project into the same research-to-wireframes-to-test sequence.

### Honest claim language

The examples below are **illustrative wording patterns**, not facts about a real project.

| Distinction | Credible wording pattern | Problematic wording |
|---|---|---|
| Individual versus team | “I designed the review interaction; engineering implemented it and the researcher led testing.” | “I delivered the end-to-end platform.” |
| Observed versus inferred | “The release removed a duplicate confirmation step. Reduced effort was an intended benefit, not measured.” | “The redesign increased productivity.” |
| Measured versus feedback | “The project lead reported easier review; no behavioral measure was available.” | “Users completed work faster.” |
| Historical evidence versus memory | “The decision note records the constraint. My explanation of the workshop is retrospective recollection.” | Presenting both as equally documented |
| Original versus reconstruction | “Portfolio reconstruction created in 2026 using fictional content; not a project artifact.” | An unlabeled polished replacement |
| Project status | “Tested prototype; not shipped.” | “The new experience” without status |

### When no outcome metric exists

Use a bounded account:

> **Illustrative wording:** “The revised flow was approved for implementation. The available record supports the change in design direction, but does not establish a post-release usability or business effect. A follow-up evaluation would examine completion, errors, and requests for assistance.”

Distinguish these possible outcomes rather than treating them as equivalent:

- A design or recommendation was produced.
- A decision changed.
- A prototype was tested.
- A feature shipped.
- Behavior changed.
- A business measure changed.
- The design caused that change.

Only claim the strongest statement the evidence supports.

### Evidence ledger

Maintain the detailed ledger privately. Give the AI-safe workspace only the approved, sanitized portion it needs.

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Claim ID | Stable identifier |
| Proposed claim | Exact wording |
| Claim type | Fact, measurement, feedback, recollection, interpretation, hypothesis |
| Attribution | Individual, team, collaborator, unknown |
| Source ID | Supporting material |
| Source location | Private reference; never automatically published |
| Source date | When the evidence originated |
| Scope | Population, product area, version, period |
| Limitations | Missing baseline, unclear authorship, incomplete records |
| Historical or retrospective | When the interpretation was formed |
| AI-processing permission | Approved, restricted, unknown |
| Publication permission | Approved, restricted, unknown |
| Public-safe wording | What may appear in the portfolio |
| Approval | Human reviewer, date, version |
| Used in | Résumé, website, presentation, application |

**Proposed rule:** no consequential public claim without a ledger entry, an authorized source basis, and human approval.

### Publishing tradeoffs

| Choice | Potential benefit | Cost or risk | Proposed decision rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conventional navigation | Predictable access | Less novelty | Default unless an alternative improves observed reviewer tasks |
| Distinctive interaction | Can demonstrate interaction craft | Discoverability and accessibility risk | Keep core navigation and evidence independently usable |
| Long-form case | Supports depth | Can bury relevance | Put summary and decision headings first |
| Concise case | Easier orientation | May omit necessary support | Link to deeper evidence or prepare discussion material |
| Motion | Can explain behavior | Distraction, loading, reduced-motion needs | Never make essential understanding depend on animation |
| Password protection | Can control access to permitted material | Access friction and failure | Use only with appropriate technical protection and authorization |
| PDF or deck | Useful for interviews and offline access | Version drift and reading-order issues | Generate from approved content and test the export |
| Custom code | Flexible behavior and reusable structure | Maintenance and release responsibility | Choose when those capabilities justify the burden |
| Simpler platform | Less implementation work | Template and platform constraints | Prefer when the evidence and reading experience fit |

Password access deserves explicit testing: McDonald reports checking failures and warns that broken access can obstruct review. That is a practical observation from one recruiter, not evidence that password protection itself is disqualifying. ([Steph McDonald interview, 2026](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/podcasts/ux-hiring-insights-steph-mcdonald-ux-design-recruiter-at-hubspot/))

## 6. Step-by-step three-tool workflow

Everything in this section is a **proposed implementation plan** built from verified capabilities. It is not a documented successful employment workflow.

### Keep facts outside conversations

Use a small, versioned handoff package:

```text
portfolio-working/
  approved/
    positioning.md
    project-inventory.csv
    evidence-ledger-safe.csv
    case-briefs/
    case-copy/
    asset-manifest.csv
  decisions/
    editorial-decisions.md
    design-decisions.md
    release-checklist.md
  handoffs/
    current-handoff.md
  public-assets/
  site/
  CLAUDE.md
```

Keep restricted originals in a **separate location outside this workspace**. The files above should contain only material authorized for the relevant processing.

Each handoff should identify:

- Approved content version.
- Approved assets.
- Claims that must not change.
- Open questions.
- Current decisions.
- Requested next action.
- Human approver.

Claude Code can load repository instructions, but its documentation treats `CLAUDE.md` as context rather than enforced configuration. Use it for conventions; use permissions and isolation for access boundaries. [Official memory documentation](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory)

### Stage 1: Define the target

**Input:** selected job descriptions, existing authorized résumé claims, time budget.

**Tool and reason:** Claude AI, for comparing text and generating questions—not choosing a career direction autonomously.

**Task:** extract recurring responsibilities, distinguish requirements from preferences, and map each to existing evidence.

**Output:** one-page positioning brief and requirement-to-evidence matrix.

**Human review:** confirm the roles are genuinely desirable and the evidence is accurate.

**Acceptance criteria:** every positioning claim points to a real project or is marked unsupported.

**Handoff:** approved brief to inventory and selection.

**Risks and stop conditions:** stop if the draft invents seniority, domain experience, or skills to match a vacancy.

### Stage 2: Audit the archive

**Input:** project list and AI-safe descriptions; originals remain outside the AI workspace.

**Tool and reason:** Claude AI, for structured interviewing and normalization.

**Task:** ask about one project at a time, especially ownership, constraints, decisions, evidence, and gaps.

**Output:** completed inventory, missing-information list, provisional ledger.

**Human review:** compare the record with actual sources and, where appropriate, authorized collaborators.

**Acceptance criteria:** uncertainty is explicit; no draft prose has upgraded memory into fact.

**Handoff:** eligible projects and evidence references to selection.

**Risks and stop conditions:** stop on unclear rights, sensitive uploads, or unresolvable attribution.

### Stage 3: Curate the set

**Input:** inventory, target-role matrix, permission status, preparation estimates.

**Tool and reason:** Claude AI, to apply the proposed rubric consistently and expose overlap.

**Task:** recommend complementary selections and explain exclusions.

**Output:** selection memo, coverage map, deferred-project list.

**Human review:** challenge the scores and examine whether a less polished case supplies stronger evidence.

**Acceptance criteria:** the set answers priority role questions without relying on duplicate stories.

**Handoff:** selected project briefs.

**Risks and stop conditions:** reject selection based primarily on recency, attractive screens, or unsupported “impact.”

### Stage 4: Approve material use

**Input:** candidate claims, assets, policies, permissions.

**Tool and reason:** human-led review first. Claude AI may organize an already sanitized checklist; it should not decide legal permission.

**Task:** separate permission to retain, process with AI, show privately, and publish.

**Output:** permission register and public-asset manifest.

**Human review:** obtain appropriate rights-holder, policy, or legal clarification when needed.

**Acceptance criteria:** every planned asset has a permitted use; restrictions are reflected in the workflow.

**Handoff:** approved sources and derivatives only.

**Risks and stop conditions:** no upload or publication while material permission remains unresolved.

### Stage 5: Draft and verify cases

**Input:** approved brief, safe ledger, authorized artifacts.

**Tool and reason:** Claude AI, for interview-led drafting, compression, and contradiction checks.

**Task:** produce summary and deep version; connect each consequential statement to a claim ID.

**Output:** case draft, claim map, uncertainty list, reviewer questions.

**Human review:** check source fidelity, contribution, voice, and whether the designer can defend the account.

**Acceptance criteria:** all consequential claims are approved; missing evidence remains visible.

**Handoff:** locked copy version to Claude Design.

**Risks and stop conditions:** reject invented quotations, research, measurements, responsibilities, or causal claims.

### Stage 6: Explore presentation

**Input:** locked copy, approved assets, page architecture, accessibility requirements.

**Tool and reason:** Claude Design, for comparing hierarchy and layout on a canvas.

**Task:** explore a small number of genuinely different presentations using identical approved content.

**Output:** homepage, project summary, and case-study direction across narrow and wide layouts.

**Human review:** choose based on reviewer tasks, not initial visual excitement.

**Acceptance criteria:** fit, contribution, status, résumé, and contact remain findable; no unapproved content appears.

**Handoff:** chosen design, tokens, behavior notes, content version, exports or verified Code handoff.

**Risks and stop conditions:** stop if decoration hides the work or generated placeholders resemble historical evidence.

### Stage 7: Choose the implementation route

**Input:** selected design, coding confidence, time, required behavior, maintenance capacity.

**Tool and reason:** Claude AI or Code in planning mode, to compare options; Code is unnecessary if using a simpler publisher.

**Task:** compare a simple platform against a small custom site.

**Output:** architecture decision with costs, limitations, update steps, and rollback approach.

**Human review:** confirm the designer can perform routine changes.

**Acceptance criteria:** an ordinary content update does not require an unexplained rebuild by an agent.

**Handoff:** implementation brief or platform-ready content package.

**Risks and stop conditions:** abandon unnecessary custom features if maintenance or deployment remains unclear.

### Stage 8: Implement

**Input:** approved content, design specification, asset manifest, architecture decision.

**Tool and reason:** Claude Code, for repository-based implementation and configured checks.

**Task:** build reusable pages, separate content from layout, implement semantic navigation and responsive behavior.

**Output:** local or protected preview, source changes, README, test configuration.

**Human review:** inspect the proposed plan, diffs, actual rendered pages, and factual consistency.

**Acceptance criteria:** approved content preserved; navigation works; production does not require a visitor’s Claude account or an AI API key in this proposed architecture.

**Handoff:** release candidate and check results.

**Risks and stop conditions:** stop on secrets, unauthorized assets, unexplained dependencies, or changes outside scope.

### Stage 9: Test and revise

**Input:** release candidate, ledger, test tasks, representative reviewers.

**Tool and reason:** Claude AI for critique hypotheses; Code for configured technical checks; humans for actual comprehension and usability evidence.

**Task:** run the distinct evaluation tracks in Section 9.

**Output:** issue log separating observed failures, automated findings, AI suggestions, and preferences.

**Human review:** prioritize issues and verify fixes.

**Acceptance criteria:** critical access, privacy, attribution, and navigation problems resolved.

**Handoff:** approved release record.

**Risks and stop conditions:** no release based only on an AI assertion that the site is accessible or “recruiter-ready.”

### Stage 10: Publish and maintain

**Input:** approved release, chosen host’s current documentation, account and domain access.

**Tool and reason:** Claude Code may prepare deployment steps; the human authorizes account access and publication.

**Task:** inspect what will become public, deploy through the chosen supported process, then test the live site.

**Output:** live portfolio, rollback instructions, maintenance checklist.

**Human review:** check logged-out access, direct links, downloads, contact, metadata, source exposure, and mobile behavior.

**Acceptance criteria:** live experience matches the approved release and contains no unauthorized material.

**Handoff:** maintenance record and application links.

**Risks and stop conditions:** stop if hosting behavior, authentication, public asset exposure, or billing is unclear.

### A bounded implementation sequence

**Planning estimate—not observed project data:** allow roughly **30–45 focused hours** for a small, template-led build with two cases, assuming authorized material is available.

| Work block | Suggested allocation |
|---|---:|
| Targeting, inventory, selection | 5–7 hours |
| Permissions and source-of-truth work | 4–6 hours, excluding external waiting |
| Drafting and factual review | 7–10 hours |
| Architecture and visual exploration | 3–5 hours |
| Implementation or platform assembly | 6–9 hours |
| Testing and release | 5–8 hours |

Permissions, missing records, or substantial technical learning can make this estimate unsuitable. Reduce scope rather than compressing factual review.

### Minimum-viable route

**Proposed target: 8–12 focused hours after material is cleared**, using a familiar platform rather than learning a complex stack.

1. Write a direct role statement and publish résumé/contact access.
2. Select one substantial case and one shorter complementary example.
3. Use Claude AI to structure approved facts and expose gaps.
4. Use a brief Claude Design exploration only if hierarchy is unresolved.
5. Assemble with an existing template.
6. Use Claude Code only for an actual implementation or defect task.
7. Obtain at least one external comprehension review.
8. Check privacy, mobile, keyboard navigation, links, and live access.

A smaller honest portfolio is the proposed response to time pressure. A rushed fictionalized account is not.

## 9. Testing and publication checklist

### Separate the evaluation tracks

| Track | What it can provide | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| AI-generated critique | Questions, possible contradictions, possible clarity problems | Actual recruiter preferences or hiring effectiveness |
| Automated technical checks | Findings from the checks actually configured and executed | Complete accessibility, usability, or factual correctness |
| Actual recruiter/hiring-manager feedback | Attributable evaluation and role-specific expectations | Universal hiring rules |
| Representative usability sessions | Observed comprehension and navigation behavior | A statistically reliable employment effect |

Do not combine these into an undifferentiated “validated” label.

### A practical reviewer study

**Proposed method:** recruit a small, purposive set of people resembling the intended audiences. Include actual recruiting or hiring experience where possible, but record each person’s relevant context rather than treating a title as universal expertise.

Give reviewers a realistic role brief and ask them to:

1. Explain what role the designer appears suited for.
2. Find a project relevant to the brief.
3. Explain the designer’s personal contribution.
4. Identify a decision and the evidence behind it.
5. Describe the outcome and its limitations.
6. Find the résumé and contact route.
7. Complete navigation on mobile or with a keyboard.

Avoid teaching the site before the tasks. Ask for explanations in the reviewer’s own words.

Record:

| Observation | Example measure |
|---|---|
| Role comprehension | Correct, partially correct, incorrect |
| Relevance discovery | Project selected and rationale |
| Attribution | Accurate distinction between personal and team work |
| Evidence comprehension | Supported versus unsupported interpretation |
| Findability | Task completion and assistance needed |
| Navigation | Blockers, wrong turns, inaccessible controls |
| Confidence | Reviewer explanation, not only a numeric rating |

Report actual counts with denominators. Do not turn a few sessions into market percentages.

### Content and readability

- Verify every consequential claim against the ledger.
- Check consistency across homepage, case, résumé, deck, metadata, and applications.
- Replace unexplained internal terminology.
- Check headings, paragraphs, captions, and image legibility.
- Ensure outcomes do not exceed the evidence.
- Keep dates and project status clear.
- Confirm quotations, if any, have authorization and a real source.
- Make the no-metrics explanation concise rather than apologetic.

### Accessibility and interaction

**Proposed target:** use WCAG 2.2 AA as the implementation brief, while making no conformance claim until an appropriate evaluation has been completed.

Check:

- Semantic headings and landmarks.
- Descriptive links and meaningful button labels.
- Keyboard access and visible focus.
- No keyboard traps.
- Reading and focus order.
- Appropriate alternative text.
- Text equivalents for information conveyed visually.
- Contrast, zoom, reflow, and narrow screens.
- Reduced-motion behavior.
- Captions or transcripts where needed.
- Form labels, errors, and confirmation behavior.
- PDF reading order and selectable text.

Do not accept an AI accessibility critique or a clean automated result as complete verification.

### Technical and loading checks

- Internal and external links.
- Direct links to every public case.
- Résumé download.
- Contact delivery and error behavior.
- Missing images and fonts.
- Browser console errors.
- Responsive layouts.
- Image dimensions and compression.
- Usability before large assets finish loading.
- Behavior if optional animation or scripts fail.
- Page titles, descriptions, social previews, and canonical URLs.
- Appropriate indexing settings.
- Error and not-found pages.
- Production build output.
- Real live-site behavior after deployment.

Set a project-specific loading budget and record test conditions. Avoid claiming that one universal loading score determines hiring success.

### Privacy, confidentiality, and authorship

Use three material classes:

| Class | Contents | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Private sources** | Restricted originals, research records, internal artifacts | Remain in an authorized environment; not automatically accessible to AI |
| **AI-safe inputs** | Minimized, approved facts and derivatives | May be processed only under the approved service/account conditions |
| **Public assets** | Approved copy and media | May be deployed and shared in the authorized form |

Review all of the following:

- Permission to publish, not merely possession of a file.
- NDA and employer-policy restrictions.
- Participant and customer information.
- Internal names, hostnames, URLs, account identifiers, and file paths.
- Screenshots, browser tabs, notifications, avatars, comments, and hidden layers.
- Image and document metadata.
- Repository history and public commit identity.
- Credentials, tokens, environment files, and logs.
- Public build output and downloadable assets.
- Preview deployments and sharing links.
- External fonts, analytics, forms, and connectors.
- AI chat links and accidentally shared workspaces.

**Anonymization is not authorization.** Neither password protection nor a fictional company name resolves an underlying publication restriction.

For protected work, require an architecture in which restricted content is not delivered to an unauthenticated visitor. A cosmetic password screen is not the acceptance criterion; test actual asset and network access before relying on protection.

### AI-assistance disclosure

Use a concise statement only if accurate, for example:

> “AI tools assisted with organization, editing, layout exploration, and site implementation. Project facts, attribution, design decisions, and publication were reviewed and approved by the designer.”

Add reconstruction labels where they occur; a general footer does not explain an individual artifact.

No résumé, employment history, identity details, or confidential project material from the UX Design Engineer workspace is used in the fictional example.

## 10. Maintenance and job-search integration

### Connect the evidence

Use the same approved claims across materials, but change emphasis and depth.

| Material | Proposed function | Consistency rule |
|---|---|---|
| Résumé | Establish relevant experience and contribution | Claims must match the ledger and portfolio |
| Application answer | Explain fit for a particular opening | Tailor selection, not history |
| Outreach | Point to a relevant example | Avoid unsupported personalization or automated mass claims |
| Portfolio | Make evidence inspectable | Keep approved canonical facts |
| Interview story | Explain choices and uncertainty | Distinguish remembered details from documented ones |
| Presentation | Support a particular discussion | Reorder and shorten cases without changing attribution |

McDonald’s 2026 account is a useful warning about automated applications that distort experience. Her acceptance of editing assistance does not extend to fabricated qualifications. ([Steph McDonald interview, 2026](https://www.careerstrategylab.com/podcasts/ux-hiring-insights-steph-mcdonald-ux-design-recruiter-at-hubspot/))

### Tailor without contradiction

**Proposed safe tailoring:**

- Reorder selected projects.
- Change the entry point.
- Emphasize a relevant decision.
- Shorten background information.
- Add a role-specific introductory note.
- Choose an authorized presentation subset.

**Unsafe tailoring:**

- Changing ownership to match the role.
- Adding methods that were not used.
- Turning a team result into individual impact.
- Removing uncertainty from the “stronger” version.
- Presenting proposed work as shipped.
- Creating incompatible dates, scope, or outcomes.

Keep the canonical project record stable. Record tailored versions as views of that record.

Record only information needed for the search, and avoid collecting unnecessary personal information about reviewers.

### Interpret progress carefully

A practical measurement chain is:

**Available → accessed → understood → discussed → advanced**

Each transition has different uncertainty.

| Observation | Reasonable interpretation | Unsupported conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Broken password reported | Access prevented review | The content was weak |
| Reviewer misunderstood ownership | Attribution needs clarification | All recruiters misunderstand team projects |
| Reviewer cited a relevant case | That case entered this discussion | The case caused advancement |
| More interviews after a revision | A temporal association worth tracking | The revision caused the increase |
| No response after applying | The application did not produce a response | The portfolio failed |

Track role fit, referrals, application timing, location or eligibility requirements, interview performance, and hiring constraints separately.

For a low-volume personal search, useful evidence is often qualitative: fewer ownership misunderstandings, clearer project selection, better questions, and fewer access failures. Do not claim a causal employment improvement without a design capable of supporting that inference.

### Maintenance routine

**Proposed cadence:**

- **Before each application:** test the exact links and access instructions being sent.
- **After useful reviewer feedback:** record the observation and update the smallest relevant area.
- **Monthly during an active search:** check downloads, contact, links, live rendering, and outdated claims.
- **After technical changes:** rerun checks and inspect the deployed result.
- **After new work:** capture approved evidence while the context is fresh.
- **At subscription or platform changes:** confirm export, ownership, access, and renewal consequences.

Require the designer to perform one ordinary content update and one rollback before calling a custom site maintainable.

### Remaining evidence gaps

- No representative 2026 study was found establishing universal portfolio review times, project counts, or format requirements.
- Current evidence is stronger for product-design and design-engineering IC roles than for research and management portfolios.
- Consumer-versus-enterprise differences cannot be reduced to one validated content formula.
- Official capability documentation establishes availability and behavior, not the quality of generated portfolio work.
- Product documentation is changing rapidly, particularly around integrated Claude Design.
- Account rollout, organizational settings, export fidelity, and actual handoff behavior still need a small local verification.
- Hosting-specific pricing, authentication, deployment permissions, and public-exposure behavior require provider-specific checks before publication.
- This report does not provide legal clearance for restricted work.
- No real reviewer sessions or employment-outcome tests were conducted.
