# What tech-company recruiters say UX designers need in their portfolios (March to October 2026)

## Findings

### Scope and strength of the evidence

The date rule (published on or after 2026-03-01) cut the evidence down sharply. Most "what recruiters want" pages for 2026 are vendor blogs that are undated, dated January or February 2026, or that name no recruiter at all (see Excluded sources in Appendix B). Within the window, first-person evidence from tech-company recruiters comes from three people. Two are current in-house recruiters: Steph McDonald, UX design recruiter at HubSpot, and Jared Tredly, design recruiter at Shopify. The third is Lena Kul, a former recruitment lead at Miro who now works as an independent design recruiter and advisor and runs PATHS. One agency recruiter, Tom Scott of Verified (an executive design recruitment firm), is reported separately below and labelled as agency. All sources are Unconfirmed. Treat every finding as a first-hand practitioner account, not a measured industry pattern. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S4] [C1]

### How recruiters screen portfolios

- **Order of review.** At HubSpot the resume comes first. The recruiter opens the portfolio only if the experience looks right for the role. [S1] [C2]
- **The recruiter is a gate, not the decider.** Tredly says he is "not making the hiring decision"; his job is to flag the candidates worth a conversation with the hiring team [S2]. McDonald describes about 300 applications narrowing to fewer than 20 conversations, with three or four candidates usually reaching the final stage. She estimates "it's probably less than 10%" of applications are viable [S1]. [C3]
- **Time spent.** No current in-house recruiter in the window gave a per-portfolio time. Kul (former in-house) describes scanning, not reading: "Nobody reads it. Everyone scans it," and a portfolio that does not catch attention "in the first seconds" is out. She adds that hiring managers may review portfolios on their phones between meetings. This is single-source. The 6-second to 3-minute figures that circulate in 2026 blogs could not be traced to a dated recruiter source in the window. [S3] [C4]
- **AI screening tools and keywords.** HubSpot uses a ranking tool that scores resumes against criteria the recruiter sets. Hard requirements can be screened out automatically with knockout questions. McDonald says she often opens low-ranked candidates first to check the ranking, sometimes disagrees with it and advances them, and that "there's still a human absolutely making the decision." An AI note-taker drafts interview scorecards but is "not making any decisions." She does not apply years of experience as an automated filter. [S1] [C5]
- **Effect on channels.** Kul describes AI filtering on both sides ("Robots are screening robots") and says this pushes hiring toward referrals, warm introductions, and people who already know your work [S3]. McDonald independently calls cold applications the weakest source of hires, behind referrals and sourcing [S1]. Two sources agree. [C6]
- **Password-protected work.** McDonald's rule is that the password must work; she suggests testing it weekly. When a password fails she emails the candidate, but warns that other recruiters may simply reject the application. [S1] [C7]
- **NDA work.** Kul: "Everyone's work is under NDA." Build what you can in public and be ready to walk through the rest privately. Showing nothing is not an option: "If you can't show me anything, don't expect to get through the process." Single-source. [S3] [C8]

### Signals that get a candidate passed to the hiring manager

- **Few, recent, relevant projects (consensus across three sources).** Tredly wants only a handful of projects, weighted to recent work, and notes that "project number 20" will not feel current [S2]. Kul says to pick "two or three: the most recent and most relevant to the role" [S3]. The agency recruiter Scott gives three to five for individual contributors [S4]. [C9]
- **Seniority visible at once.** Tredly says seniority shows straight away in layout, spacing, and presentation. Senior candidates should show influence over larger, ambiguous problems rather than small refinements; his example of the small kind is a "two-step cart refinement." Single-source. [S2] [C10]
- **Reasoning and impact over raw numbers (consensus in direction, different emphasis).** Tredly calls numbers without context "death by numbers" and asks "Why do you think it was better?" Candidates without post-launch metrics, such as agency designers, can still advance if they explain their reasoning [S2]. McDonald looks for craft, cohesion, impact, and the journey of the product, including "the messy stuff at the beginning" such as wireframes and first versions [S1]. Kul says companies want craft combined with business impact, influence over engineering and product, and comfort with ambiguity [S3]. [C11]
- **Visual craft.** McDonald says strong visual design matters for every HubSpot design role [S1]. The agency recruiter Scott calls weak visual craft the number one red flag [S4]. [C12]
- **Format.** About 90% of the portfolios McDonald sees are websites. Slide decks and Notion or Coda pages are fine if polished, but an uncurated Google Drive folder counts against the candidate. A video walkthrough supplements written impact; it does not replace it. Single-source. [S1] [C13]

### Red flags

- **Portfolio red flags.** Tredly names overstuffed case studies (a 75-slide deck makes him ask "What are they trying to tell me?"), dated work, and decisions without reasons [S2]. McDonald names noisy hover effects and interactions that add nothing [S1]. Kul names portfolios built for the designer instead of the reader [S3]. [C14]
- **AI misuse in the application.** McDonald's red flags are pasted ChatGPT output that still contains the chatbot's follow-up text ("we can tell you didn't even audit the response from AI"), auto-apply bots that apply to the wrong discipline, a tool-rewritten resume that turned a product manager into a "lead designer," and batches of identically formatted resumes she suspected were fake. She advises against auto-apply tools. Single-source, but first-hand. [S1] [C15]

### AI skills and AI-generated portfolios

- **Using AI to polish your own writing is acceptable.** McDonald does not mind it. [S1] [C16]
- **AI skill as a criterion: little from in-house recruiters.** Neither current in-house recruiter in the window names AI skill as a portfolio screening criterion. Kul (former in-house) says companies increasingly want designers who can "build and prototype with AI," that most postings do not name specific tools, and that companies want judgment about where AI belongs. She expects juniors to be "much better with the tools than an average senior." She adds that AI makes weak design easy to produce, so the gap between good and mediocre work shows more. No in-window tech-company recruiter addressed fully AI-generated portfolios directly. Thin evidence. [S3] [C17]
- **Agency recruiter view (kept separate).** Scott (Verified, agency) says companies he works with score AI as its own competency, that interview tasks increasingly say "prototype this with AI," and that no visible AI use is a portfolio red flag. His claim that AI knowledge is "required in 99% of companies now" carries no source. [S4] [C18]

### The market from the recruiter's side

- **Fewer openings, more applicants, a senior tilt.** McDonald says teams that once had 5 to 15 design openings a year now have one or two. Requests are shifting toward senior and staff roles, which makes things harder for early-career candidates. Postings can close within hours after drawing hundreds of applications. HubSpot usually fills a role in 30 to 45 days but will wait longer for the right person [S1]. Kul calls the market "hostile," with "hundreds of applications" per role [S3]. Two sources agree. [C19]
- **Juniors and seniors are judged differently.** Kul says juniors are judged on potential, learning speed, and taste; passion projects count, and a made-up impact story is not needed. Seniors are judged on scope, influence, business impact, and craft [S3]. McDonald screens out new graduates for senior roles by hand [S1]. [C20]
- **Agency view on process (kept separate).** Scott reports shorter, sharper hiring processes with fewer stages before the onsite [S4]. Designer-hosts of the Design Table Podcast, discussing his breakdown, report that take-home tasks and paid work trials are returning [S6]. These are second-hand for the trials point. [C21]

## Conflicts

1. **How much AI decides who gets screened.** A Muzli guide (vendor, Unconfirmed) states "78% of recruiters use AI-assisted screening to filter portfolios before a human ever opens your site" and cites no source; 2026 blogs repeat the figure [S5] [C22]. The only in-house recruiter in the window who describes her tooling (HubSpot, Unconfirmed) says a ranking tool advises, a human decides, and she checks low-ranked candidates by hand [S1]. Effect: the report does not treat AI pre-screening as a dominant gate. It reports one company's practice and flags the 78% figure as unsourced.
2. **How much process to show.** McDonald (HubSpot, in-house, Unconfirmed) wants to see early, messy work [S1]. Tredly (Shopify, in-house, Unconfirmed) prefers one well-reasoned part of a flow to a full start-to-finish process [S2]. Scott (agency, Unconfirmed) says lead with outcomes, not process [S4]. Effect: the shared ground is "show reasoning and impact concisely" (C11). How much process to include is left as a real disagreement.
3. **Metrics.** Scott (agency, Unconfirmed) says lead every case study with outcomes [S4]. Tredly (Shopify, in-house, Unconfirmed) warns against numbers without explanation and says candidates without metrics can still pass on reasoning [S2]. Effect: C11 is worded around explained impact, not metrics alone.
4. **Whether AI skill is a hard requirement.** Scott (agency, Unconfirmed) treats visible AI use as required and its absence as a red flag [S4]. Kul (former in-house, Unconfirmed) says companies want AI prototyping ability, but most postings do not name tools [S3]. The two current in-house recruiters do not list it as a screening criterion [S1] [S2]. Effect: AI skill is reported as rising in importance and as an agency-led claim, not as a consensus screening rule.
