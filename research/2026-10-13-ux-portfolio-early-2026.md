# What hiring voices said UX portfolios needed, January-February 2026

## Findings

### Scope and how much was usable

The report covers only sources published from 2026-01-01 through 2026-02-28. I opened or date-checked 31 items. Eight fell inside the window. Five of the eight are usable evidence: NN/g [S1], two Figma research posts [S2] [S3] and two newsletter posts by an executive design recruiter [S4] [S5]. Two in-window items exist mainly to sell courses and back no claim (X1, X2), and one is a link roundup with no claims of its own (X3). The other 23 items were outside the window or undated (X4 to X24).

Most of what the searches turned up about UX portfolios was marketing. Of the 31 items, 15 come from publishers that sell what the advice leads to: portfolio builders, bootcamps and courses, coaching or mentorship, or a staffing agency. Almost all the specific portfolio rules (case-study counts, review times, AI-screening rates, password advice) came from this marketing layer, and nearly all of it falls outside the window. The in-window evidence is mostly about skills and market conditions. Little of it is about portfolio content itself.

### Research organizations

**Market state.** NN/g reports that the UX job market began to stabilize from late 2024 through 2025 after the 2023-2024 drop. It says recovery is uneven, with entry-level roles still "scarce and highly competitive", and expects 2026 to stay competitive [S1] [C1]. NN/g's piece is editorial: it reports no original data and cites UXPA salary surveys and User Interviews only for the point that team sizes look stable [S1].

**Hiring-manager demand.** Figma's hiring study surveyed "design managers around the world." In it, 82% said their organization's need for designers had increased or held steady (47% increased, 35% same, 18% declined). 40% planned more headcount in the next six months, but only 20% thought the overall hiring landscape was improving [S2] [C2]. The post gives no sample size, countries, field dates or vendor. The full study is a gated download [S2]. The same post passes on a Designer Fund estimate that design postings across its portfolio companies rose about 60% in 2025. That is a third-party figure with no method shown [S2] [C19].

**Seniority.** In Figma's study, 56% of hiring managers reported rising demand for senior hires and 25% for junior roles [S2] [C3].

**Skills in demand.** Figma's hiring managers most often put visual polish among the five most important designer skills (58%). More than 45% named collaboration, systems thinking and product strategy [S2] [C10]. 73% see a growing need for candidates skilled in AI tools, and 79% for knowledge of designing AI products [S2] [C6]. NN/g lists research, stakeholder management, leadership and design craft as core skills. It adds working with AI, designing AI experiences and systems design as skills to keep acquiring, and says human direction, curation and verification remain essential [S1] [C8]. It expects roles to "demand breadth and judgment, not just artifacts", favors adaptable generalists, and says practitioners who only assemble design-system components are "replaceable by AI" [S1] [C12].

**Designer-side survey.** Figma's State of the Designer 2026 is the one in-window study with a disclosed method: an online survey of 906 digital designers across North America, APAC, Europe, LATAM and the Middle East, fielded by the research firm NewtonX in seven languages [S3]. Designers most often defined craft as visual polish (58%), then thoughtful problem solving (47%) [S3] [C13]. 91% of AI adopters said AI tools improve their designs, and AI users were 25% more likely to report job satisfaction [S3] [C18].

**Publisher interest (Figma).** Figma sells design software, including AI features. Its AI findings fit its product interest: hiring managers want AI-tool skill, and AI users are happier. Neither finding requires buying Figma specifically. The designer survey's method is disclosed. The hiring study's method is not disclosed in the post, so its percentages are single-source, unaudited figures from an interested publisher. Its non-AI findings (seniority, visual polish, demand) do not depend on the product.

### Design managers

No in-window source let design managers speak at length about portfolios in their own words. Their views reach this report in two ways. The first is Figma's survey of design managers (C2, C3, C6, C10 above). Its post quotes one design manager, who said they still hire for "evergreen skills" such as usability and cross-functional facilitation [S2]. The second is client feedback relayed by an agency recruiter (next section), which is secondhand.

Taken together, the in-window record says hiring managers in early 2026 were:

- favoring senior over junior candidates [S1] [S2] [S5] [C5];
- treating AI fluency as expected rather than optional [S1] [S2] [S4] [S5] [C9];
- still weighting visual craft heavily [S2] [S4] [S5] [C17].

**Seniority differences.** For leaders, Tom Scott reports that companies want "designers and leaders who love doing IC work" and that leaders "can't hide behind playbooks" [S4] [C20]. No in-window source described how leadership portfolios are reviewed differently from IC portfolios.

### Recruiters

**In-house tech-company recruiters.** I found no in-window source written by, or directly quoting, an in-house recruiter or talent partner at a tech company. So this report has no in-window evidence on how in-house recruiters screen, what they pass on or reject, whether they use AI to screen portfolios, or how they handle password-protected or NDA work. The sources that cover these topics are out of window, undated or vendor content (see Excluded sources, especially X6, X7, X11, X12, X13, X15, X16).

**Agency recruiter: Tom Scott (Verified).** Tom Scott is an executive design recruiter whose business, Verified, places designers. His posts are anecdotal and drawn from client feedback and his own searches. He gives no counts [S4] [S5]. His portfolio guidance:

- For ICs, three to five focused case studies. Lead with outcomes, "show the work, don't describe it", and put recent work ahead of older, more polished projects [S5] [C14].
- Red flags: describing interactions that were never built, leaning on generic process frameworks such as the double diamond, strategy that was mainly done by a PM, and committing to a first direction without exploring alternatives [S5] [C15].
- Visual craft is "non-negotiable at IC level." Candidates with a narrow UX background and no well-crafted visual work "will and do struggle to get through the portfolio review for IC roles." He frames this as an observation, not an endorsement [S4] [S5] [C11].
- Hiring is potential-based. The design exercise exposes gaps between what candidates say and what they produce [S4] [S5] [C16].
- AI fluency is "the sharpest dividing line" and "no longer a 'nice-to-have'." Companies want designers who own more of the frontend, work in dev environments and ship [S4] [S5] [C7].
- It is "insanely tough for junior designers", and most companies want senior talent [S5] [C4].

Who benefits: Scott's posts promote his paid newsletter, not a portfolio product. His advice does not require buying anything he sells, so the posts are kept as evidence. Because they are single-person anecdote, every claim resting only on them is marked weak in the claim map.

### Consensus and single-source claims

- **Consensus (independent sources agree):** senior candidates are favored over junior ones [C5]; AI fluency is now expected [C9]; visual craft still counts heavily with hiring managers [C17].
- **Single-source:** every portfolio-specific rule in this report (case-study count, outcome-first structure, red flags, the role of the design exercise) comes from one agency recruiter [C14] [C15] [C16]. All Figma percentages come from one survey whose method is undisclosed [C2] [C3] [C6] [C10]. NN/g's market description is editorial, without data [C1].

### Statistics traced

- The 82%, 73%, 79%, 56%/25% and 58% hiring-manager figures trace to Figma's hiring study via its blog post [S2]. Later secondary pages repeat them (X4, X7, X8, X9, X10). The primary study is gated, and the post gives no sample size.
- The 906-designer survey traces to [S3] with method disclosed.
- The ~60% design-postings rise is Designer Fund's estimate, reported by Figma [S2]. No primary publication or method was found.
- "78% of recruiters use AI-assisted screening" appears only in vendor content (X5, March 31, out of window), with no source. It also conflicts with an undated vendor survey (X12) reporting that 71% of respondents' organizations do not use AI to review portfolios. Neither figure is used here.

## Conflicts

1. **Craft output versus judgment.** Tom Scott [S4] [S5] (Unconfirmed) says companies care about "output, output, output!" and that weak visual craft blocks IC candidates at portfolio review. Figma's hiring managers [S2] (Reputable) rank visual polish as their most common top-five skill. NN/g [S1] (Official) says roles will "demand breadth and judgment, not just artifacts" and warns that component assembly is replaceable by AI. These can be reconciled: visual craft gets a candidate past the portfolio screen, and judgment is what NN/g sees as lasting value. But the sources weight them differently. The findings report both, and treat visual craft as the stronger near-term screening signal because two independent sources support it.
2. **Generalists versus specialists.** NN/g [S1] (Official) predicts adaptable generalists will thrive. Excluded out-of-window vendor content (X5, X6) says hiring managers prefer niche specialists. No in-window source supports the specialist position, so the findings follow NN/g and note the disagreement.
3. **Direction of the market.** Figma's hiring managers mostly report steady or rising demand, yet only 20% see the overall landscape improving [S2] (Reputable). NN/g calls 2026 competitive, especially for juniors [S1] (Official). These agree on stabilization without broad improvement. The findings present the market as stable but tight.
