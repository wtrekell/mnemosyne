# What design managers say UX designers need in their portfolios (March to October 2026)

## Findings

### Scope and strength of the evidence

This report covers only what design-side hiring people say. It leaves out tech-company recruiters and research organizations, which other reports cover. Every source was published on or after 2026-03-01. Each source's date is in Appendix B.

The evidence is thin. Searches found few first-person posts by named, currently hiring design managers dated after 1 March 2026. Most of the material is one of these:

- a design leader's essay (Julie Zhuo [S1]);
- a design leader's podcast whose hiring segment is paywalled, so it is known only through a secondary write-up (Jenny Wen, head of design for Claude at Anthropic [S2] [S3]);
- newsletters that pass on feedback from hiring managers (Tom Scott [S4] [S5]; Jeff Humble [S6]);
- job postings written by hiring teams [S9] [S10];
- a survey of design leaders [S11].

Tom Scott [S4] [S5] needs a lens note. He says he has 10+ years of hiring designers, but he now runs Verified, a design talent platform, and one podcast calls him a recruiter [S8]. His posts are used here because they report what hiring managers at client companies told him. Read them as reported manager views, not as one manager's first-person account. No source passed the citation test, so every non-posting source is labelled Unconfirmed.

Sources found but excluded:

- **Published before 2026-03-01:**

### What portfolios must show

**Decision-making and rationale over process ritual.** This is the most consistent theme. Reviewers want to see the reasoning behind the work: trade-offs, what was cut, and whether a feature should exist at all. They do not want a walk through a standard method [S1] [S4] [S5] [S6] [S7] [C1]. Zhuo contrasts designers who "optimize the artifact" with those who optimize for the user [S1]. Humble suggests one 500–800 word narrative that traces a single decision from ambiguity to outcome [S6].

The matching red flag is the process-heavy case study: the Double Diamond used as the structure, long blocks of text, and visuals with no context [S4] [S5] [S6] [S12] [C2]. Scott puts it bluntly: "Process-heavy portfolios are done" [S5].

**Shipped, live, traceable work.** Managers favour work that is live and can be checked. Animated wireframes, concept redesigns and unshipped prototypes count for less [S4] [S5] [S6] [C3]. Humble reports that Harry Enaholo, a hiring manager who reviewed 150+ portfolios for a Design Lead role in March 2026, screened on whether products were live and scaling. This is second-hand; the original LinkedIn post was not found [S6].

**Outcomes first.** Case studies should open with the result: what changed, what shipped, and what it affected. Metrics should be quantified where possible [S4] [S12] [S7] [C4]. Hiring-team postings ask for this directly. LinkedIn's Staff posting asks for "quantified business and user outcomes," and its AI posting asks how you measure success [S9] [S10].

**Visible AI use in the process.** Managers now treat AI use as a baseline. A portfolio with no AI signal reads as a sign that the designer has not adapted [S4] [S5] [S6] [S7] [S10] [C5]. Scott reports that AI skill is now scored as its own competency next to UX, visual design and systems thinking [S4]. In the Designer Fund survey, 50% of leaders said they put more weight on AI fluency when hiring [S11]. LinkedIn's Staff posting asks candidates to include AI prototyping work and describe how they used AI, but lists it as preferred, not required [S10].

What counts is AI used with judgment: show what you changed in the AI's output. Generic AI-made portfolios "look average" and read as sameness [S6] [S7] [S12] [C6].

**Designing AI products.** This is mostly evidenced by postings. LinkedIn's conversational-AI posting asks candidates to show:

- interfaces for "non-deterministic outcomes";
- their workflow with AI research scientists and engineers;
- how they measure AI feature effectiveness [S9] [C7].

It is a single employer's posting, so treat it as one data point.

**Visual craft as a gate.** Scott calls weak visual craft "the number one reason people don't get interviews" [S5]. He reports that craft deep dives test typography, spacing, hierarchy and interaction [S4]. Only 5% of leaders in the Designer Fund survey said they now weight execution quality less [S11]. Design Table's hosts argue that UI craft matters more as AI-generated products flood app stores [S8]. Together these make craft a gate [C8].

Scott adds that pure-UX portfolios with no visual evidence struggle [S4] [C9]. Only one source makes that claim.

**Interactive prototypes.** Clickable or coded prototypes, Figma links and video walkthroughs beat static screens [S4] [S7] [S12] [C10]. Jess Eddy, a coach and not a manager, favours "scrappy, functional prototypes" tied to 3–6 month horizons [S7]. Postings name code-based prototyping with Copilot or Figma Make as a preferred skill, and Framer and ProtoPie for AI-driven prototypes [S9] [S10].

**Ownership and role clarity.** Say exactly what you did. Team work presented as your own, and vague "I led" claims, are red flags [S5] [S12] [S1] [S9] [C11]. Zhuo says designers who hand impact and strategy to others "cannot ask for the title of excellent" [S1]. In interviews, probing exposes strategy that was really done by a PM [S4]. Strong candidates "remember the texture of the pain": what failed, what was cut, what they owned [S1] [C12].

**Collaboration.** Most often this comes up as partnership, not a separate portfolio section. The conversational-AI posting asks candidates to detail how they work with AI researchers and engineers [S9]. Scott reports that leaders are judged on partnership with product and engineering more than on arguing for design's seat at the table [S4]. Zhuo values teams that "confront users early" [S1] [C20].

### Curation and form

Use few, deep, recent case studies. Sources converge on 3–5 [S6] [S10] [S12]. Zhuo frames the portfolio as a "trailer," not a museum, whose first job is "intrigue" rather than completeness. Her red flag is "tidy grids of projects with generic text" [S1] [C13]. Uxcel's reviewer quote, "Your portfolio is as strong as your weakest case study in it," supports cutting weak work [S12].

### How the portfolio review and presentation are judged

Scott reports fewer, sharper loops: a portfolio review and a craft deep dive before the onsite, often with a practical task to prototype with AI and talk through the thinking [S4]. Humble reports some first rounds that replace the walkthrough with a live 25-minute Figma problem [S6] [C14].

Only Zhuo [S1] describes how the presentation itself is judged [C15]. She looks for:

- "an aesthetic in economy": complex work distilled without being flattened;
- "ball control": keeping the discussion on the right topic;
- the ability to "telescope into the details."

Her warning sign is a walkthrough that "feels like a dance," which suggests the candidate has little to say about the project [S1].

### Differences by seniority

**Staff and Principal.** Scott's scope-title mismatch flag targets Staff+ candidates whose senior titles rest on feature-level work. He reports that Staff portfolios should show multiplier work, such as patterns adopted by other teams, and "work before the work," such as alignment and problem framing [S5]. A portfolio of isolated screens signals screen-level thinking [S4]. LinkedIn's Staff posting asks for platform-level and design-system thinking and for mentoring [S10], and its Senior AI posting asks for systems thinking [S9] [C16].

**Head of Design and Director.** Scott reports that companies want hands-on player-coaches who show measurable gains in design quality, and treat being hands-off as a red flag [S4] [C17]. Only one source says this.

**New grads and juniors.** Wen hires a "cracked new grad" archetype: a fast learner with "almost a blank slate" [S3] [S2]. Eddy advises early-career designers to present projects as "science projects" [S7], and Zhuo reads side projects as evidence of appetite [S1] [C18].

**Specialists and generalists.** Wen hires deep specialists and "block-shaped" generalists, meaning people about 80th-percentile good at a few core skills [S3]. Polly D'Arcy, VP of Design at Wealthsimple, "hires specialists over generalists," but only the episode's topic list was reachable [S13]. Humble reports that Enaholo's strongest applicants were the most specific [S6] [C19].

### Consensus vs. single-source claims

Several sources agree on four things:

- reasoning over process ritual [C1];
- shipped work [C3];
- visible AI use with judgment [C5] [C6];
- craft as a gate [C8].

Six things rest on one source each:

- the pure-UX penalty [C9];
- hands-on Heads of Design [C17];
- presentation criteria [C15];
- AI-product portfolio content, from one employer's posting [C7];
- Wen's archetypes, through a secondary write-up [C18] [C19];
- D'Arcy's specialist preference, from a topic list only [C19].

Taste is contested; see Conflicts [C21].

## Conflicts

1. **Is taste a differentiator?** Jenny Wen, as quoted second-hand, says "we might be holding on to taste as a differentiator a little bit too much" [S3] (Unconfirmed). Many leaders in the Designer Fund survey say taste and judgment matter more now that AI makes "good enough" output easy [S11] (Unconfirmed) [C21]. Effect: the report treats visual craft as a gate [C8] but does not claim taste alone sets candidates apart.

2. **Polish vs. work in progress.** Scott says the portfolio should be treated as a product, with craft as the first filter, and that a template better than the work is a red flag [S4] [S5] (Unconfirmed). Humble says unpolished, in-progress work can show how a designer thinks [S6] (Unconfirmed). Zhuo puts intrigue ahead of completeness [S1] (Unconfirmed). Effect: the findings say craft gates entry and leave open how much unfinished work to include.

3. **How much text?** Humble suggests a 500–800 word decision narrative [S6] (Unconfirmed). Scott lists "reams of text" as a red flag [S5] (Unconfirmed). Uxcel suggests about 30% text to 70% visuals [S12] (Unconfirmed). Effect: all three agree decisions must be visible [C1], but they disagree on length.

4. **Is AI prototyping required?** Scott reports that AI skill is required at "99% of companies" [S4] (Unconfirmed). LinkedIn's Staff posting lists Figma as required and AI prototyping as preferred [S10] (Official). Effect: C5 is stated as a baseline expectation, not a hard requirement.

5. **Specialists vs. generalists.** D'Arcy [S13] and Enaholo, as reported in [S6], favour specialists (both Unconfirmed). Wen hires both kinds [S3] (Unconfirmed). The Designer Fund survey reports less focus on narrow specialization [S11] (Unconfirmed). Effect: C19 reports the split and does not take a side.
