# Psychological effects of color on humans

Scope notes. Sources are limited to peer-reviewed journals, universities, NIH/PMC and standards bodies, as requested. Several were read only as abstracts or search-result summaries, not full text; those are marked "abstract-level" in the source list. Where a claim could not be sourced to a qualifying source, it is flagged "UNSOURCED". Evidence from North America (US/Canada) is noted where I could establish it; otherwise the report says so.

## Findings

### 1. Overview: how strong is the field?

The field is young and methodologically uneven. A review by Elliot (a leading researcher of the topic) says most studies fail to control color at the spectral level, often leave viewing conditions (distance, ambient light, background) uncontrolled, and are frequently underpowered, which inflates effect sizes. Most theory addresses hue only; lightness gets some attention and chroma is "almost entirely overlooked". Moderators (culture, gender, age, task) are understudied [S1] [S2] [C1].

Rough ranking of the evidence found:

- Better supported: perceptual and legibility effects (luminance contrast), color-vision-deficiency constraints, the isolation (distinctiveness) effect on memory, structure of color preference, and the fact that hue, saturation and brightness each shape felt emotion in lab ratings.
- Contested or weak: red impairing intellectual performance, red/blue approach-avoidance priming, red boosting contest wins, red boosting attractiveness.
- Not established: that any particular hue has a fixed psychological effect independent of context.

### 2. Performance and cognition

**Red and intellectual performance: weak.** A meta-analysis pooling 67 effect sizes from 38 samples found no meaningful red effect on anagram or knowledge tests, a small effect only on reasoning tests, much larger effects in early studies than later ones, and no evidential value after correcting for publication bias [S3] [C2]. Four replications by the same research group, using the word "red" as a prime, also found no effect on verbal reasoning or general knowledge tests (abstract-level; year not verified) [S4] [C2].

**Red/blue approach-avoidance priming: failed replication.** A direct replication of Mehta and Zhu's red/blue anagram result, with 263 students versus 69 in the original and enough power to detect the original effect, found nothing [S5] [C3]. Elliot's review also notes studies that did not support red's effect on cognitive performance [S1]. The original authors' account and the replicators' accounts differ on whether the procedures matched; I saw this only in conference posters, which I did not use as sources, so it is not resolved here.

**Red in contests: effect gone in newer data.** Hill and Barton's 2005 Nature finding that red wearers won more in combat sports was re-tested by a 2024 meta-analysis (Hill and Barton among the authors) of 6,589 outcomes from seven Summer Olympics (1996-2020) and nine World Boxing Championships. Red won 50.5% overall (not significant) and 51.5% of close contests; pre-2005 close contests had shown 56.8%. The authors suggest awareness of the effect and rule changes may have removed it [S6] [C4].

### 3. Emotion, physiology and light

**Hue, saturation and brightness all matter.** In a lab study (N = 62, 30 colors on an LED display, 30 s each) arousal rose with saturation and brightness and varied by hue, increasing from blue and green toward red. Valence was highest for saturated, bright colors, and blue's valence advantage appeared only at high saturation. Saturated and bright colors produced stronger skin conductance; chromatic colors briefly sped heart rate [S9] [C7]. This is one study with a small sample. The host is Mainz, so the sample is probably German, but I did not verify this.

**Color-emotion associations are broadly shared, with local variation.** A 30-nation study (4,598 participants, 22 languages; 20 emotion concepts by 12 color terms) found strong cross-national agreement (mean r = .88), with additional variation by nation that was smaller between linguistically or geographically close nations [S10] [C8]. These are associations people report for color terms, not measured effects of color on behavior. I could not retrieve the country list, so whether and how strongly the US and Canada are represented is unverified.

**Light versus surface color.** Blue-enriched light raises alertness through melanopsin-containing retinal cells, but this literature concerns light, mostly at night. Daytime studies are fewer, and studies of white light with different correlated color temperatures are mixed [S13] [C18]. Elliot's review links blue light to alertness and attention-task performance [S1]. Do not generalize this to blue paint, screens' interface colors or printed material.

### 4. Preference

Color preference is best explained, in one US sample, by what people associate with the color. The ecological valence theory (Palmer and Schloss, 2010) predicts 80% of variance in average ratings of 32 colors using the valence of objects people associate with each color, beating color-emotion (55%), color-appearance (60%) and cone-contrast (37%) models. Preference peaks at blue and dips at chartreuse; saturated colors beat muted ones; dark yellow and dark orange (olive, brown) are least liked [S11] [C9]. Evidence is correlational, only 32 monitor-displayed colors, and the preference-rating sample was 48 volunteers, mostly from the San Francisco Bay Area, with some Berkeley undergraduates. Whether preferences are innate or learned is unresolved [S11].

Sex differences in hue preference are contested: an early English/Chinese comparison found similar differences in both groups, a later comparison of British and Himba (Namibia) observers argued they vary by culture, and a published commentary rejects that conclusion [S12] [C10]. None of this evidence is North American.

### 5. Perception and attention (single colors)

Color helps distinctiveness. Making an item stand out by color strongly enhances its memory (the isolation or von Restorff effect), and red enhanced memory for negative words while green enhanced it for positive words [S19] [S20] [S21] [C16]. Caveats reported in secondary summaries: samples are mostly student populations, short retention intervals, and single isolated items (abstract-level; not verified) [C16].

### 6. Region (North America)

- US evidence found: the Palmer and Schloss preference data (San Francisco Bay Area/Berkeley) [S11] and the Steele replication (Appalachian State University, a US institution; student sample) [S5].
- Canada: no Canada-specific study was identified or verified.
- Everything else is non-North-American, mixed or unverified: the 30-nation study (US/Canada inclusion unverified) [S10], the Mainz lab study [S9], Hill/Barton contest data (international Olympic athletes) [S6], the British/Himba/Chinese preference work [S12]. Treat these as limitations; none establishes a North American pattern [C19].

## What changes with color combinations

**Hue similarity, preference and harmony.** Pair preference and pair harmony both rise as the hues become more similar. Preference depends more strongly than harmony on how much people like each component color and on lightness contrast. Preference for a figure's color against its background goes the other way: it rises with hue contrast with the background. So "which pair is liked", "which pair is harmonious" and "which figure color stands out well on a ground" are three different questions [S14] [C11]. The dataset behind the component-color finding is the Berkeley preference work above [S11]; the pair study's population was not verified.

**Simultaneous contrast and context.** A color's appearance shifts with its surround. Experimental work attributes induced color shifts to lateral interactions between neural color mechanisms, and shows stronger induction from brief than from long presentations (abstract-level) [S15] [C12]. Color-in-context theory generalizes this idea to meaning: the same hue is read differently by context (blue as a first-place ribbon versus on meat), but this is a theory, and its headline predictions (red) are the contested ones in section 2 [S1] [C6].

**Legibility and accessibility.** This is the best-grounded combination effect. WCAG success criterion 1.4.3 requires a 4.5:1 luminance contrast ratio for normal text and 3:1 for large text (18 pt, or 14 pt bold). The 3:1 value comes from ISO 9241-3 and ANSI/HFES 100-1988, and 4.5:1 compensates for the contrast sensitivity loss with about 20/40 vision. Contrast is calculated from luminance, so hue is not a key factor; the page advises avoiding red against black because of protanopia [S16] [C13] [C14]. Color-vision deficiency affects men far more than women per the NEI; the commonly cited figure of about 8% of males is secondhand here and unverified [S18] [C14]. Nielsen Norman Group guidance holds that low-contrast text is illegible and inaccessible; it is expert guidance and I found no measured reading-speed data in it [S17] [C15].

**Attention and memory with multiple colors.** Isolation by color works because one item differs from its neighbors [S19] [S21] [C16]; the corollary that many differently colored items dilute the effect is plausible but I found no source that tests it (UNSOURCED). Color's role in guiding visual search (Wolfe and Horowitz, Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2004) is the standard review, but I did not read its conclusions, so I make no claim from it.

**Learning materials.** Studies on "emotional design" found warm colors and round shapes evoke positive emotion that can aid motivation and learning. Manipulating color harmony (complementary, similar, contrasting) did not change learners' emotional state, had a minor learning effect and affected intrinsic motivation; results overall are mixed, and pleasant colors that do not fit the learning goal had null or harmful effects (summary-level, from secondary descriptions) [S22] [C17].

**Color-emotion when hues are combined.** UNSOURCED. I found no qualifying source on how emotion associations of two or more hues combine, as distinct from preference and harmony [C20].

## Conflicts

1. **Does red enhance attractiveness?** Lehmann, Elliot and Calin-Jageman's meta-analysis found small effects (d = 0.26 men rating women; d = 0.13 women rating men), with heterogeneity, evidence of shrinkage over time and smaller effects in preregistered studies [S7, Unconfirmed]. A set of nine preregistered student replications found no effect [S8, Unconfirmed]. The adversarial collaboration ended with Elliot disputing the quality of included studies and others calling the effect small, perhaps nonexistent (search-level report, not primary). I report the effect as small at best and contested [C5].
2. **Red and performance.** Early studies report large effects; meta-analysis and direct replications do not [S3, S4, S5, all Unconfirmed]. Elliot's review acknowledges non-supporting studies but presents the theory as viable [S1, Unconfirmed]. A red-pen study (Fikrlová 2019) found more failure-related word completions; it appeared in one search summary only and is not cited as a source here.
3. **Sex and culture in hue preference.** See section 4: positions in [S12, Unconfirmed]; unresolved [C10].
